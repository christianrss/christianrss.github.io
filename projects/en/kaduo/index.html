Kaduo — Real-Time Media and Social Commerce

Browser video production, live media delivery and commerce-aware interactions.

Back to projects · Português

Architecture overview

Kaduo brings social commerce, video publishing and live shopping into one product, while treating media delivery and business transactions as distinct domains. The public description follows logical responsibilities rather than exposing infrastructure topology.

  • Creator experience: device capture, video preview, live visual composition and production controls.
  • Media workflow: media publishing, ingestion, processing or forwarding, delivery and playback.
  • Commerce workflow: sellers, catalogs, featured products, transactions and customer interactions.
  • Real-time experience: live conversation, audience-facing events and status updates.
  • Data and operations: product state, creator settings, event analysis and operational observability.

These domains are connected by application interfaces, but video transport is conceptually independent from the order lifecycle.

Real-time video and production studio

The production studio supports camera and microphone capture and live composition. Visual elements such as titles, images, product information, promotional graphics and audience interactions can be arranged while the creator is broadcasting. Rendering occurs in the media-production workflow so that supported visual elements become part of the outgoing video rather than only the creator's preview.

The broader platform includes recording and publishing shorter videos, live sessions, playback experiences and controls for broadcast quality. Protocol-based media transport such as WebRTC supports interactive publication. Specific network routes, provider configurations, credentials, deployment topology and operational parameters are intentionally not published.

Design choices and trade-offs

Client-side composition: reduces the need for a dedicated server-side graphics renderer for each creator session, but requires careful performance and browser-compatibility engineering.

Separate media and commerce responsibilities: live video can evolve independently from catalog and checkout rules. The cost is coordinating state that viewers see with the media timeline.

Dedicated real-time interaction channel: audience events and studio controls should not rely on the bandwidth and lifecycle of the video stream alone.

Protocol-oriented integrations: open media standards improve interoperability, while external services introduce platform and operational dependencies.

Alternative architectures

A server-side compositor could provide more controlled rendering and advanced production workflows, at the cost of computational capacity and increased operations. A fully managed broadcast stack would simplify infrastructure ownership but reduce control over the authoring experience. An external-encoder-only approach would suit professional studios while excluding some creators who prefer broadcasting directly from a phone or browser. These are architectural comparisons, not claims about the project's development history.

Quality strategy and evidence

The private codebase includes unit tests around portions of media lifecycle handling and event processing. Their existence was reviewed, but the complete product test suites, throughput and end-to-end broadcast reliability were not independently executed or measured for this public case study.

A sound validation program covers capture permissions, supported browsers and devices, composition correctness, playback, network interruptions, recording and upload recovery, cross-device interaction and accessibility. Published measurements should include test methodology, hardware and network conditions; this page intentionally does not claim specific latency, uptime or audience capacity.

Boundaries and maturity

Media quality depends on capture hardware, browser capabilities and network conditions. Interactive graphics create a trade-off between visual richness and smooth frames. Distribution, storage and business operations have different scaling characteristics, so each requires independent capacity planning.

The architecture described here is a technical overview. It is not a public audit of internal controls, dependencies, security posture or production service levels.
